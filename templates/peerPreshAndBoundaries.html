Peer Pressure & Boundaries

Practical ways to speak up for what feels right to you

Practical Refusal Scripts & Exit Strategies

Direct refusals: “Nah, that’s not really my thing” or “I’m good, thanks.”

External excuses: “My parents track my location and test me” or “I have an early game tomorrow and can’t risk it.”

Delay or pivot: “I’m starving, let’s grab food first” or “Let’s talk about this later.”

Safety text: Agree on an emoji or code phrase with a trusted person that means you need an immediate, no-questions-asked pickup.

Types of Pressure & Recognizing Them

Direct: Explicit demands, dares, or repeated requests.

Indirect: FOMO, social cues, or feeling like “everyone else is doing it.”

Digital boundaries: You can set limits on location sharing, instant replies, private photos, and group-chat drama.

Academic pressure: You can refuse to share homework, allow copying, or join group cheating while still being respectful to friends.

Boundary-Setting Frameworks

Traffic light limits: Green means comfortable; yellow means you need time or have conditions; red means a hard no.

Clear communication: “I’m okay with [X], but I’m not comfortable with [Y].”

When someone pushes back: Repeat your boundary calmly, avoid getting pulled into a debate, and leave or ask for support if the pressure continues.

Interactive Tools & Downloads

Scenario simulator: Choose a response to a realistic situation and see possible outcomes.

Script cards: Saveable phone wallpaper cheat-sheets with quick scripts for party, digital, or school situations.

Friendship check-in: A brief self-assessment to consider whether your social circle respects your choices.

Healthy Friendships & Getting Help

Green flags: Supportive friends accept “no,” respect your privacy, and do not punish you for making your own choices.

Red flags: Friends use guilt, threats, repeated pressure, or private information to get their way.

Stepping back: Spend less time with pressuring people, keep plans simple, and lean on other supportive friends without starting a confrontation.

Get help: Talk to a counselor, trusted adult, or peer advisor if pressure becomes harassment or you feel unsafe.
